- If you live on or below a slope, take simple steps to identify landslide hazards and reduce your risk.

## Maps
- Geologic maps (state geological survey or USGS); surficial geology maps often document landslides.
- Landslide inventory maps: incomplete in much of the U.S.; absence of a mapped landslide does not mean there is no hazard.
- Landslide susceptibility maps: show where a landslide may start; often do not show runout, which can threaten people on flat ground below a slope.

## Community resources
- Planning department: ask about local landslide hazards, codes, hazard maps, referrals to local geologists/engineers.
- Conservation districts (some have landslide expertise).

## Preparedness on your property
- Stormwater: find where gutter, driveway and other runoff flows. Water concentrated onto a slope can saturate soil and raise landslide risk. Consider piping it to a storm sewer, road ditch or other drainage (check local restrictions first).
- Native vegetation: keep native plants on slopes; roots help stabilize soil. Removing them can increase susceptibility; fast-growing non-native species often reduce stability.
- Yard waste and debris: do not dump yard waste or debris on a slope (smothers vegetation, adds weight).
- Professional evaluation: in areas with known landslide problems, hire a geologist or engineer experienced with landslides.
- Emergency preparedness: emergency kit and household evacuation plan including pets (links to Ready.gov).
- Neighbors: work with neighbors on or below a slope; landslides do not respect property lines.
